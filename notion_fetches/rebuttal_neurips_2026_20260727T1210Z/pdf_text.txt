Abstract
Industry-scale order-flow foundation models, including TradeFM [Kawawa-
1
Beaudan et al., 2026] and MarS [Li et al., 2025a], are now being trained with
2
token counts comparable to large language models, yet without a well-defined rule
3
for how parameters and data should be jointly scaled. We show that importing
4
language-model data-budget heuristics into order-flow modelling can lead to severe
5
under-training. Fitting an additive Chinchilla-style scaling law to a Mamba-3 width
6
sweep on tokenised order-flow message streams, we recover a markedly different
7
compute-optimal allocation: near C ≈1020 profiled FLOPs, the optimum lies
8
at tens of order messages per parameter – about 30 messages or 780 tokens per
9
parameter. The fitted data exponent, β ≈1.33, is nearly five times the Chinchilla
10
value for natural language (β ≈0.28), producing a highly asymmetric loss surface
11
in which under-sampling data is penalised sharply. These results suggest that data
12
budgets for order-flow foundation models cannot be inherited from language-model
13
heuristics, even though the compute-optimal path still couples more data with rapid
14
parameter growth.

Introduction
16
Limit-order-book (LOB) data is one of the most abundant high-resolution sources of sequential data
17
outside natural language. NASDAQ data [Huang and Polak, 2011] alone records hundreds of billions
18
of order messages across the listed US equity universe, and the totals across other exchanges and
19
other asset classes extend the picture further. Once tokenised, these corpora can reach into the trillions
20
of tokens, comparable in scale to the data budgets used to pretrain modern open-weight language
21
models, opening the door to training very large autoregressive models on order-flow streams.
22
Large autoregressive models for this area face the same allocation question that motivated language-
23
model scaling laws: at a fixed training compute budget, how much should be spent on model
24
parameters and how much on additional observed tokens? The answer cannot be imported directly
25
from natural language. LOB data has stronger local syntax constraints, heavy temporal autocorre-
26
lation, long-tail noise, and a hardware cost profile that is not well captured by the textbook 6ND
27
approximation, especially for Mamba-style state-space kernels and multi-node training.
28
This paper reports a Mamba-3 logged-loss scaling law. The analysis uses ten Mamba-3 SISO logged-
29
loss curves, maps each checkpoint to Cij = q(Ni)Dij using a profiled FLOPs/token curve, and fits

the additive Hoffmann-style surface Lij = E + A/N α

31

Background
53
Limit order book and LOBSTER.
A limit order book records every active resting buy and
54
sell order at one equity on an electronic exchange. Orders at each price level are matched in
55
price-then-time priority, with the best bid and best ask defining the bid–offer spread. Under the
56
continuous double-auction protocol used by NASDAQ, the book evolves through a stream of typed
57
message events: a new limit order (Type 1) added to the book if it is non-marketable; a partial
58
cancellation (Type 2) reducing the size of an existing resting order; a full deletion (Type 3) removing
59
an existing resting order; and an execution (Type 4) matching a resting order against incoming
60
liquidity. Hidden executions, trading halts, and cross trades are excluded following Nagy et al. [2023].
61
The LOBSTER reconstruction [Huang and Polak, 2011] of NASDAQ TotalView–ITCH provides a
62
publicly distributed canonical form of this stream at nanosecond resolution per ticker per day, and the
63
eight-equity sub-corpus used in this paper (Section 3.1) contains roughly 3.09 × 1010 such messages
64
over 2022–2025.
65
From a raw LOBSTER record to a 26-token block.
Each raw LOBSTER record carries a typed
66
mixture of categorical fields (event type, side) and numerical fields (price, size, timestamps, optional
67
reference order ID), so an LOB stream cannot be tokenised in the byte-pair sense used for text. We
68
adopt the field-disjoint tokenisation of Nagy et al. [2023], originally an LOBS5 22-token block over a
69
vocabulary of |V | = 2,112 IDs, and extend it to 26 tokens per message via two sequential digit-splits
70
that remove the deep-book price truncation of the original scheme; only a negligible fraction of size
71
clipping remains, and it is disclosed in the preprocessing notes. The resulting per-message layout
72
is shown in Appendix Figure 6, with positions grouped into core message fields, inter-arrival time,
73
absolute time, and reference fields used by cancel/modify/execute messages; the full vocabulary
74
structure and the position-wise field decomposition are deferred to Appendix C. Throughout the paper
75
the data axis D is therefore counted in these 26-token units, not in raw messages or optimiser steps.
76
Scaling-law functional form and the profiled compute axis.
Following Hoffmann et al. [2022]
77
we describe the per-token cross-entropy as
78
L(N, D) = E + A N −α + B D−β,
with N the total parameter count including embeddings (the convention reconciled by Pearce and
79
Song [2024]), D the number of tokens seen, E the irreducible loss floor under our tokeniser,
80
context, and model family, and α, β > 0 the parameter- and data-side exponents. Turning this
81
surface into a compute-optimal allocation requires fixing a compute axis. The textbook closed
82
form C ≈6ND [Kaplan et al., 2020, Hoffmann et al., 2022] ignores tokeniser overhead, attention-
83
quadratic terms, and state-space kernels; we instead use a profiled compute axis C = q(N) D, where
84
q(N) is a FLOPs/token profile measured once per model size from GPU profiler counters (NVIDIA
85
dmon/GPM) during training (Appendix G). After this definition, all compute budgets are reported as
86

Logged loss, validation CE, and IsoFLOP slices.
The fitted loss in this paper is the logged
93
training loss, interpreted as a Chinchilla-style pretraining loss in the sub-epoch regime. Hoffmann et
94
al. explicitly model final pretraining loss using smoothed training loss, noting that it is an unbiased
95
estimate of test loss when the number of training tokens is smaller than the full corpus size [Hoffmann
96
et al., 2022]. Our sweep is in the same one-pass accounting regime: the largest run consumes fewer
97
encoded token occurrences than the 2022–2025 LOBSTER corpus contains. We therefore treat logged
98
training loss as the relevant same-distribution pretraining/test loss for the scaling fit. Chronological
99
and instrument-held-out cross-entropy are stricter deployment-shift checks, not prerequisites for
100
fitting the same-distribution scaling surface. An IsoFLOP valley, finally, is the locus of (N, L)
101
obtained by interpolating loss curves across runs at a fixed C = q(N)D; a parabola in log L over
102
log N then identifies the valley minimum N ⋆
iso(C) and gives a slice-level cross-check on the global
103
additive fit [Czech et al., 2026]. The current paper reports the logged-loss fit and IsoFLOP analysis,
104
while keeping held-out CE as a deployment validation target.
105
Selective state-space models and Mamba-3.
The architectural family used throughout this paper
106
is the selective state-space model line, in which a recurrent hidden state is updated by an input-
107
dependent transition rather than a fixed kernel. Mamba [Gu and Dao, 2023] introduces this selectivity
108
over diagonal SSMs; Mamba-2 [Dao and Gu, 2024] reformulates it through state-space duality so
109
that selective recurrences run as hardware-efficient blockwise matrix operations during training while
110
preserving constant-memory inference; and Mamba-3 [Lahoti et al., 2026], the variant fit in this paper,
111
additionally applies trapezoidal discretisation, rotary position embeddings on the input-dependent
112
B/C projections, and BCNorm. We use the SISO output variant exclusively (Section 3.2); the MIMO
113
variant and a Transformer counterpart are listed only in the FLOPs/MFU profile of Appendix G. The
114
Mamba-3 block is plugged into a dual-stream message/book/fused outer wrapper at depth allocation
115
(Nmsg, Nbook,pre, Nbook,post, Nfused) = (2, 1, 1, 6); the full schematic of the stream merge and shared
116
sequence-layer wrapper is given by Figure 4 in Appendix A.

3
Methodology
118
3.1
Training corpus and held-out evaluation
119
We train on 8 NASDAQ large-cap equities from LOBSTER [Huang and Polak, 2011]: GOOG, AAPL,
120
NVDA, AMZN, META, TSLA, MSFT, and AMD over 2022-01-01 to 2025-12-31. This corpus
121
contains approximately 3.09×1010 messages and, under the 26-token-per-message encoding adapted
122
from Nagy et al. [2023], approximately 800B (8.03 × 1011) tokens at a fixed context of 500 messages
123
(13,000 tokens) per sample.
124
The validation protocol is forward-in-time on January 2026 data, covering all eight in-distribution
125
equities and two out-of-distribution equities, MU and NFLX, that are held out by instrument.
126
This held-out stream defines the validation target for the planned scaling law. The fitted Eval would
127
estimate the per-token validation cross-entropy floor for the specified setting: NASDAQ LOBSTER
128
order flow, 500-message context, 26-token encoding, and the chosen Mamba-3 model family.
129
3.2
Architectures and width sweep
130
The reproduced scaling analysis uses only Mamba-3 SISO. The available logged-loss curves comprise
131
10 long runs, ranging from 8M to 197M parameters, using the 26-token message encoding and a
132
500-message context. The Mamba-3 configuration matches the state-space backbone of Lahoti et al.
133
[2026] with SISO output, RoPE on the B/C projections with rope-fraction 0.5, expand factor 2, and
134
state dimension 128. We report the total parameter count N on every plot axis.

3.4
Uncertainty in the reported figures
154
The uncertainty bands in Figures 1 and 2 are percentile bootstrap intervals, not standard deviations,
155
standard errors of the mean, or symmetric Gaussian error bars. The grey parametric band resamples
156
temporally correlated logged-loss residuals along the observed training curves under a fixed (Ni, Dij)
157
design, while the red IsoFLOP band resamples retained fixed-compute valley rows. These bands
158
quantify sensitivity of the plotted fits to the observed logged-loss data; they do not include seed-
159
to-seed variation, new training runs, chronological or instrument-held-out validation-loss noise,
160
FLOPs-profile uncertainty, or misspecification of the additive Chinchilla form. We therefore interpret
161
disagreement between the global additive surface and local IsoFLOP valleys as model-specification
162
uncertainty rather than compressing it into a single headline combined CI. Appendix B gives the
163
bootstrap procedures.

3.5
Scaling-law variables and empirical fit scope
165
The sweep starts from logged LOB training curves rather than from a pre-built scaling-law table.
166
Each retained checkpoint supplies a tuple (Ni, Dij, Lij), and the analysis is only interpretable once
167
the three axes have fixed meanings.
168
We therefore use total parameter count for N, cumulative encoded tokens for D, profiled compute
169
C = q(N)D, and logged training loss for L. These choices put all runs on the same axes while
170
keeping the claim scoped to the logged-loss surface, not to a held-out validation law.
171
The same accounting choices also determine how uncertainty should be read. The grey band resamples
172
within-run logged-loss residuals, whereas the IsoFLOP band resamples fitted valley rows; neither
173
band is a seed-replication or new-run interval. Appendix E gives the full checklist and explains how
174
these conventions connect to the methodology critiques in Section 5.

4.3
Held-out validation status
222
The scaling law above follows the Chinchilla convention: it models the same-distribution pretraining
223
loss using smoothed logged training loss in a regime where the consumed training tokens are fewer
224
than the corpus tokens available. Under this one-pass accounting, training loss is the scaling-law loss
225
for the cached pretraining distribution, as in the original compute-optimal language-model analysis
226
[Hoffmann et al., 2022]. The forward-time and instrument-held-out CE pass asks a different question:
227
whether the scaling shape and compute allocation survive the deployment shifts that matter for
228
order-flow modelling.
229
Those shifts are substantive. First, training examples drawn from the same trading day are highly
230
overlapping, so consecutive logged losses have a smaller effective sample size than the raw token
231

234
instruments can reduce same-distribution pretraining loss while failing to transfer to unseen tickers.
235

but not time, is therefore only a description of the cached training population. The logged-loss
247
exponents should be treated as deployment-relevant only if the held-out surfaces reproduce the same
248
allocation trend on both the forward in-distribution stream and the instrument-held-out stream.
249
4.4
Marginal cost of loss reduction
250
Under the profiled compute convention C = q(N)D, the fitted logged-loss surface implies
251
N ⋆(C) ∝C0.849,
D⋆(C) ∝C0.236,
Lr(C) = L(C) −E ∝C−0.314.

Thus the remaining reducible logged loss falls slowly with compute: halving it would require about
252
9× more compute, and a decade of additional compute would reduce it by only about a factor of two.
253
This is a local logged-training-loss estimate, not a held-out validation result.
254
The same frontier also shows why there is no fixed tokens-per-parameter rule. Since
255
D⋆(C)/N ⋆(C) ∝C−0.613,
the fitted data-per-parameter ratio decreases with compute. Ratios such as “messages per parameter”
256
are therefore local summaries at a chosen compute budget, not constants of the scaling law.
257
All such statements refer to the fitted parametric surface. They should not be read as claims about the
258
empirical best-so-far frontier, which locally falls below the fitted projection in Figure 1.

5
Related Work
260
The foundation model paradigm, which has transformed natural language processing, has begun to
261
be adapted for the financial domain. At the aggregated price-and-return level, this includes general
262
time-series foundation models [Ansari et al., 2024, Woo et al., 2024, Das et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2024,
263
Goswami et al., 2024, Garza et al., 2023, Rasul et al., 2024] and the finance-specific Kronos [Shi et al.,
264
2025]. The most granular level of market data, however, is the Limit Order Book (LOB) itself, where
265
the LOBSTER dataset [Huang and Polak, 2011] for the S&P 500 alone amounts to many billions of
266
order messages, which, once tokenised, is comparable in scale to the corpora used to train modern
267
foundation language models such as LLaMA-3 [Grattafiori et al., 2024]. Earlier deep-learning work
268
on LOB data spans CNN-based models [Zhang et al., 2019, Briola et al., 2025], GANs [Coletta et al.,
269
2021, Cont et al., 2023], diffusion models [Backhouse et al., 2025, Berti et al., 2025, Wang and Ventre,
270
2026], and recurrent networks [Hultin et al., 2023]. Recent work has increasingly focused on large
271
autoregressive foundation models trained on tokenised message sequences, which seem to outperform
272
alternative paradigms on the LOB-Bench distributional benchmark [Nagy et al., 2025]. LOBS5 [Nagy
273
et al., 2023] introduced token-level next-token prediction on tokenized LOBSTER data with an S5
274
backbone [Smith et al., 2023]; MarketGPT [Wheeler and Varner, 2024] and TradeFM [Kawawa-
275
Beaudan et al., 2026] adopt Transformer backbones, with TradeFM scaling to multi-asset-class trade
276
flow; ByteGen [Li and Chen, 2025] operates directly in byte space for a lossless representation; and
277
MarS [Li et al., 2025a] and LOBERT [Linna et al., 2025] both compress each message into a single
278
token to obtain longer effective contexts, the former within a market simulation engine, and the latter
279
via a BERT-style encoder.
280
How parameters and data should be scaled jointly is a central question for training such large models.
281
Kaplan et al. [2020] established power-law scaling for autoregressive language models, and Hoffmann
282
et al. [2022] introduced the now-standard additive functional form with an irreducible-loss floor.
283
The same form has since been shown to hold across a wide range of modalities: image, video, and
284
mathematical problem solving [Henighan et al., 2020]; general time-series tokens [Edwards et al.,
285
2024, Yao et al., 2025]; tokenized world-model data [Pearce et al., 2024]; symbolic music tokens [Qu
286
et al., 2024]; motion forecasting and planning [Baniodeh et al., 2025]; and genome-scale nucleotide
287
sequences [Nguyen et al., 2024], with exponents that depend strongly on tokenizer and architecture.
288
Analogous studies report scaling for selective state-space models [Gu and Dao, 2023, Dao and Gu,
289
2024], gated linear recurrences [De et al., 2024], and the xLSTM family [Beck et al., 2026]. The
290
fitting methodology has correspondingly been sharpened by replication and reconciliation work:
291
Besiroglu et al. [2024] critique overly narrow confidence intervals; Pearce and Song [2024] reconcile
292
Kaplan and Chinchilla by making parameter-counting conventions explicit; Porian et al. [2024] isolate
293
the cosine-LR-decay endpoint and warmup-scaling as dominant sources of the Kaplan–Chinchilla
294
discrepancy; Li et al. [2025b] survey reporting standards across the field; and Czech et al. [2026]
295
characterise structural biases in IsoFLOP parabola fits. We address these critiques in our methodology
296
(Section 3) and report the current Mamba-3 LOB-message scaling law as a reproducible step toward
297
a strict validation scaling law.

6
Conclusion
299
This paper provides a first scaling-law estimate for tokenised order-flow generation under a measured
300
Mamba-3 compute model. Fitting an additive Chinchilla-style surface to logged training loss, we find
301
that order-flow scaling differs sharply from the language-model heuristic: the fitted data exponent is
302
much larger, and the local compute-optimal allocation corresponds to tens of order-flow messages
303
per parameter rather than a fixed token-per-parameter rule. This suggests that current order-flow
304
foundation models may be undertrained at their reported model scales, and that the optimal allocation
305
depends sensitively on the compute convention used to define the frontier.
306
The reproduced Mamba-3 sweep gives a coherent logged-loss scaling law, but its allocation frontier
307
is tied to the profiled compute model. With C = q(N)D, the fitted optimum scales as
308
N ⋆(C) ∝C0.849,
D⋆(C) ∝C0.236,
rather than following the standard Chinchilla allocation implied by C ∝ND. Thus, the main
309
empirical result is not a universal “tokens per parameter” constant, but a profiled allocation frontier
310
for this architecture, tokeniser, corpus, and compute range.
311
Deployment.
This training-time prescription does not require serving the largest trained model
312
in a latency-sensitive trading stack. If inference speed, memory footprint, or co-location cost is the
313
binding deployment constraint, the natural follow-up is to train near the compute-optimal allocation
314
and then distill the resulting autoregressive order-flow policy into a smaller student model, including
315
online policy distillation while the teacher continues to generate or score market continuations. In
316
that use case, the scaling law specifies where to spend training compute, while distillation determines
317
the deployable inference footprint.
318
Limitations.
Several limitations are important for interpreting the scaling figures. First, the
319
reproduced fit is based on the available eight-stock training corpus, corresponding to roughly 800B
320
encoded tokens. We have not yet tested whether the same law holds at larger token budgets, on later
321
forward-in-time streams, or across a broader universe of equities. In particular, the current exponents
322
may partly reflect the liquidity, volatility, event-rate, and tick-size regimes of the stocks used in the
323
sweep. Projections beyond this corpus should therefore be read as extrapolations from the current
324
logged-loss surface, not as validated large-scale or cross-instrument scaling predictions.
325
Second, the sweep covers only a limited range of model sizes. The current runs span 8M–197M
326
parameters, which is enough to reveal a local allocation trend but not enough to establish a broad
327
asymptotic scaling law over multiple decades of N. This matters especially for the projected compute-
328
optimal frontier, since the fitted exponents are being used to extrapolate beyond the densest region of
329
observed runs.
330
Third, the longest runs still do not fully populate the large-D regime needed to test the far end of the
331
fitted data exponent. Longer training runs, or a dedicated matched-compute sweep with more evenly
332
spaced checkpoints at high token counts, would be needed to check whether the same D-dependence
333
persists further out.
334
Finally, the current sweep does not include enough independent seeds at each model size to estimate
335
seed-to-seed or new-run variance. The bootstrap bands quantify within-run logged-loss residual
336
variation and IsoFLOP valley-row sensitivity, but they should not be interpreted as uncertainty over
337
fresh training runs, architecture choices, or new data samples.

Strictly, the stochastic-approximation term

639
Training-curve geometry of the curvature.
A complementary reading recovers the same fixed-C
640
conclusion without further parametric assumptions. Each fixed-N training curve in the log C–log L
641
plane bends down toward its own loss floor E + AN −α as D →∞, and is tangent to the FLOP-loss
642
frontier at a single compute-optimal point. Whether a single training curve effectively contributes
643
a long or short segment to the frontier depends on the curvature of the frontier near that tangency:
644
when the frontier is locally flat (as a curved-frontier prediction approaches the asymptote) a single
645
curve’s tail tracks the frontier across a wider C window, since the loss penalty for departing from
646
N ⋆(C) along that window is small; when the frontier is locally steep, the same fixed-N curve falls
647
below the frontier rapidly off-tangency, and a larger N must take over to maintain tangency. A given
648
C range on the frontier is therefore approximated, in a slope-only picture, by a sequence of training
649
curves at increasing N (multiple model-scale-ups, each contributing a short segment), and, in the
650
curved-frontier picture, by fewer training curves at smaller N extended to larger D (each contributing
651
a longer segment). The shift in modal large-language-model practice from successive scale-ups (e.g.
652
GPT-2, GPT-3, MT-NLG) toward extended-token training of fixed-or-shrinking N [Hoffmann et al.,
653
2022, Grattafiori et al., 2024] tracks this geometric distinction; it is the same observation as the
654
Appendix-E remark, restated in the language of training-curve coverage rather than frontier-slope
655
arithmetic.

657

658

661

662
Parameter axis.
We report N as the total parameter count, including embeddings and unembedding.
663
This follows the accounting convention clarified by Pearce and Song [2024]. The choice matters
664
because the fitted surface and the compute-optimal allocation both place model size directly on the
665

666
Data and compute axes.
The data coordinate is tokens seen, not optimizer steps. This matters
667
in the present sweep because different runs can have different batch sizes, sequence lengths, or
668
accumulation factors. Counting D as cumulative tokens keeps those checkpoints comparable without
669
an extra step-based rescaling.
670
The compute coordinate is then built from the profiled FLOPs/token curve,
671
C = q(N)D.
Here q(N) is measured once per model size from GPU profiler counters during training (Appendix G).
672
This is the compute convention used in both the parametric frontier and the IsoFLOP slices.

Loss axis.
The loss coordinate is logged training loss, specifically the checkpointed step_loss.
674
It is not strict chronological or instrument-held-out validation cross-entropy. Logged loss is useful
675
because it is available densely along every run, but it can carry learning-rate-schedule effects and
676
within-run autocorrelation [Porian et al., 2024]. The fitted E is therefore the asymptote of this
677
logged-loss surface, not a validated entropy floor.
678
Fit and uncertainty.
The surface fit uses the additive Hoffmann form with a log-Huber objective
679
on log residuals, with δ = 10−3. This keeps the fit tied to the multiplicative scale on which the
680
residual bootstrap is later defined.
681

682
differs by figure. For the grey surface band, the design points (Ni, Dij) are fixed and length-20
683

684

686

Training protocol
740
This appendix consolidates the training-protocol details (shared schedule, two-track Muon-AdamW
741
optimiser, compute-axis measurement, and IsoFLOP-slice construction) that the scaling-law analysis
742
of Section 3.3 treats as fixed inputs. The current analysis assumes these optimiser choices transfer
743
across the width sweep; it has not verified an optimiser-invariance window with learning-rate
744
ablations.
745
Shared schedule and token budget.
The reproduced runs use global batch size 128 sequences,
746
weight decay 5×10−3, and a 1%-warmup cosine learning-rate schedule whose denominator coincides
747
with each run’s target step count. The fit itself uses recorded tokens seen, Dij, so differences in
748
effective tokens per step are handled through the data column rather than through a fixed step
749
multiplier.
750
Two-track Muon-AdamW optimiser.
2D weight matrices are trained with Muon [Liu et al.,
751
2025, Jordan et al., 2024] at a fixed ηMuon = 10−2 across the entire width grid; embeddings, the
752
unembedding/decoder, layer-norm gains, and biases (the AdamW auxiliary path, ≤5% of total
753
parameters and shrinking with d) use a flat ηAdamW = 5 × 10−4.
754
Width invariance of Muon.
Muon orthogonalizes the gradient by Newton-Schulz iteration and
755
rescales by
p
max(fan-in, fan-out), producing a per-step activation update bounded independently
756
